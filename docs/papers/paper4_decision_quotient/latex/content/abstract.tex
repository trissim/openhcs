Which coordinates of a decision problem can you hide without changing the optimal action? The answer depends on how the question is posed. We show that exact relevance certification has fundamentally different complexity in three natural settings: static (ruling out counterexamples), stochastic (comparing conditional expectations), and sequential (preserving policies over time). For a decision problem $\mathcal{D}=(A,S,U)$, this yields the decision problems \textsc{Sufficiency-Check}, \textsc{Minimum-Sufficient-Set}, and \textsc{Anchor-Sufficiency}, together with the optimizer quotient as the coarsest abstraction that preserves optimal-action distinctions.

The central result is a sharp complexity escalation across regimes. Surprisingly, the search for a minimum sufficient set in the static regime collapses to relevance containment, placing it in coNP rather than the expected $\Sigma_2^P$. In contrast, stochastic and sequential formulations are harder: stochastic sufficiency is PP-complete, stochastic anchor and minimum queries sit at the existential counting boundary with PP-hardness and paper-level $\textsf{NP}^{\textsf{PP}}$ upper bounds, and sequential sufficiency, minimum, and anchor queries are PSPACE-complete. Under standard assumptions, this yields a strict hierarchy: static $\subset$ stochastic $\subset$ sequential.

We prove an encoding-sensitive dichotomy where the tractable-hard boundary is sharp, and we identify an integrity-competence trilemma: no exact certifier can simultaneously be sound, complete, and polynomial-budgeted on the hard regime. We also isolate six structural conditions under which exact certification remains polynomial. This establishes both tractability islands and fundamental limits for exact simplification.
